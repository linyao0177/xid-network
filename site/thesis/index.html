The xid thesis

Every machine will become a witness.

Meters, sensors and machines already decide who gets billed, who gets paid and what gets believed. This is our case for how their word becomes something the whole economy can rely on.

I

The witnesses

Somewhere right now a meter is counting the kilowatt-hours a rooftop sends to the grid. A probe in a shipping container is logging four degrees. A pump is recording how much water it lifted, a charger how much energy it sold, a sensor on a street corner how much soot is in the air.

None of these machines thinks of itself as a witness. But that is what they have become. Their readings settle invoices, trigger insurance claims, back carbon credits, decide whether a shipment is accepted and whether a subsidy is paid. More and more of the economy's ledgers are written by machines, and more and more money moves on what they say.

We think this is the start of something much larger. As devices get cheaper and connections get easier, machine testimony will become the default record of what happened in the physical world. The question is whether anyone will be able to believe it.

II

The weak link is in the middle.

Follow a reading from the sensor to the payment and you find the same thing almost every time: a server that nobody else can inspect. The device reports to a platform. The platform stores the number, shows it on a dashboard and sends it to whoever pays. Everyone downstream takes the platform's word for what the device said.

That arrangement has three costs we have learned to live with. It can be wrong without anyone knowing. A number can be changed, lost or withheld between the device and the bill. It is a single point of failure. When the platform goes down, every device behind it goes quiet. It is an island. Each vendor's devices speak only to that vendor's cloud, so data that should be shared stays locked inside the company that collected it.

We accepted all this because there was no other way that was cheap enough. Checking machine data independently meant expensive hardware, special networks and a lot of people. So we trusted the middle, and the middle grew.

The question is no longer whether machines can talk. It is whether we can hold them to their word.

III

Trust has always been infrastructure.

Whenever a society found a way to make trust cheap, a new economy grew on top of it. Merchants who could check each other's books could lend to strangers. Ports that agreed on one box could ship anything anywhere. Computers that agreed on one protocol became the internet, and an internet that could encrypt became a place to shop. A ledger that nobody controlled made it possible to send money without a bank in between.

Each of these did the same thing. It took a question that used to need a trusted middleman (is this record true?) and answered it with a rule that anyone could check. We believe machine data is next.

1494

1956

One standard box. Ships, trains and trucks trust the same unit; global trade follows.

1983

One protocol for every network. Machines trust the same rules; the internet follows.

1995

2009

Bitcoin

Now

Machine testimony

Devices that sign and nodes that check. Anyone can trust a reading; a machine economy follows.

IV

Why now: three lines finally meet.

An open network for machine data was not possible ten years ago. It needed three things to become cheap at the same time, and each of them has.

Chips that sign

A microcontroller that costs a few dollars can now hold its own key and sign every reading. On an ESP32-C3 it takes 21 milliseconds. Proof of origin no longer needs special hardware.

Networks without servers

Peer-to-peer networking can reach a device behind any home router or mobile network, with no public address and no cloud in between. A device can find the network on its own.

Money machines can hold

Stablecoins on programmable chains can pay for a single unit of work, automatically, to whoever did it, anywhere. A network can pay its operators per result instead of per contract.

V

Five principles for machines we can believe.

1

Sign at the source.

A reading is born with its device's signature and a sequence number that only rises. From then on, nobody can change it, replay it or claim the device said something else.

2

Check by many.

Every piece of work is done by several independent nodes and watched by others. No single operator's word is ever enough on its own.

3

Pay on agreement.

A smart contract pays for a result only when enough nodes signed the same one. Agreement is the product; it is also the only thing anyone gets paid for.

4

Make lying expensive.

Every operator puts up a stake. Sign a false result and the others recompute it, prove it and take part of that stake. Honesty becomes the profitable strategy.

5

Own nothing in the middle.

Anyone can run a node, devices find nodes by themselves, and the contract's admin can hand control to its verifiers. There is no company that has to stay in business for the network to work.

VI

What becomes possible.

When a machine's word can be checked by anyone, the paperwork around it starts to disappear. The reading becomes the invoice, the evidence and the trigger at once. Here is what we think that opens up.

Energy

Your roof sells to your neighbour.

The meter's signed readings are the invoice. Independent nodes agree on the bill and the power stays on while it is paid. We have run exactly this on a microcontroller, end to end.

Cold chain

The box proves the vaccine never warmed.

A temperature probe signs every reading on the journey. The buyer, the carrier and the insurer read the same record, and nobody has to take anyone's word for it.

Climate

Evidence that was never self-reported.

A project's emissions data comes signed by the devices that measured it and checked by strangers with stake on the line, instead of a spreadsheet sent by the project itself.

Machines for hire

A machine bills by the hour, by itself.

A compressor, a charger or a drill rents out its time. The bill is computed from its signed usage and two of three independent nodes must agree on it before anyone pays.

What a signature cannot do. It proves which device spoke and that nobody changed its words. It cannot prove the sensor measured honestly. That is why xid starts identity at the factory, where a manufacturer vouches for each device, and why the most valuable data will come from many devices that can be checked against each other.
VII

A network that outlives its makers.

Infrastructure people trust has to be neutral, and it has to last longer than any one company. That includes us. So xid network is designed to need us less over time: open code, nodes anyone can run, operators paid by the work itself, and a contract whose admin key can be given up so that verifiers govern it instead.

We are not there yet, and we would rather say so plainly. Here is the road as we see it.

Done

Prove it works

The whole network runs on a public testnet with a real microcontroller as the meter. In tests 12 nodes settled 99.7% of work and a lying node was slashed within a minute.

Next

Make it cheap

Settle one proof per node per hour instead of every unit. Our model takes a device's cost from about $42 to about $1.54 a month.

Then

Make it safe

A third-party audit of the contracts, real meters and cellular devices in the field, and a mainnet launch.

Finally

Hand it over

Renounce the admin key. From then on the network's verifiers, weighted by stake, are the only ones who can change it.

The machines are already talking. Now they can be held to their word.

If you build devices, run infrastructure or depend on machine data, we would like you to help build this with us.
